How to find your electric rate on your bill

The calculator needs your electric rate in dollars per kilowatt-hour ($/kWh). Utility bills rarely show one simple number, because the price is split into several charges. Here are two easy ways to work it out.

Method 1: the all-in rate (easiest)

  1. Find the electricity used for the billing period. It's listed in kWh, often near a usage graph or a "meter reading" section. Example: 800 kWh.
  2. Find the total electric charges for the same period. If your bill also includes gas, water, or old balances, use only the electric part. Example: $120.
  3. Divide the dollars by the kWh: $120 ÷ 800 kWh = $0.15 per kWh.

This number includes taxes, fees, and delivery charges, so it shows what you really pay on average. It includes a fixed monthly customer charge too, which makes it a little higher than the true cost of each extra kWh. For a rough estimate, that's fine.

Method 2: add up the per-kWh charges

Look in the detailed charges section. You'll often see lines like:

  • Supply or generation: the cost of the electricity itself, shown as a price per kWh.
  • Delivery, distribution, or transmission: the cost of getting it to your home, also per kWh.
  • Smaller per-kWh riders or surcharges.
  • A fixed customer or service charge (dollars per month). Leave this one out, because it doesn't change with usage.

Add the per-kWh prices together. If a price is shown in cents (for example 9.5¢), divide by 100 to get dollars ($0.095).

Things that can change your rate

  • Tiered rates: the price goes up after you pass a certain amount of usage in a month.
  • Time-of-use rates: electricity costs more during "peak" hours, often late afternoon and evening. If you're on one of these plans, use the rate for the hours you actually run the appliance.
  • Seasonal rates: many utilities charge different prices in summer and winter.

Bills can vary from month to month, so averaging two or three bills gives a steadier number.

Can't find it?

Your utility's website usually has a "rates" or "tariffs" page listing the current prices for your plan, or you can call customer service. As a last resort, the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) publishes average residential prices for each state. The calculator's default is a U.S. average placeholder, so replace it with your own number for a better estimate.

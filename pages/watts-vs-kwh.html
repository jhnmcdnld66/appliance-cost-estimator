Watts vs kWh explained

Appliance labels list watts. Your electric bill charges for kilowatt-hours (kWh). They're related, but they measure different things.

Watts measure power, right now

A watt (W) measures how fast an appliance uses electricity at a given moment. A 10-watt LED bulb uses electricity slowly; a 1500-watt space heater uses it 150 times faster. One kilowatt (kW) is 1000 watts.

Kilowatt-hours measure energy, over time

A kilowatt-hour is the energy used when something draws 1 kilowatt for 1 hour. It's the "amount" of electricity, and it's what the utility meter counts.

A driving analogy helps: watts are like your speed (miles per hour), and kWh are like the distance you've driven (miles). Driving fast for a short time can cover the same distance as driving slowly for a long time.

The formula

kWh = watts ÷ 1000 × hours

  • A 100 W TV for 10 hours: 100 ÷ 1000 × 10 = 1 kWh.
  • A 1500 W heater for 1 hour: 1500 ÷ 1000 × 1 = 1.5 kWh.
  • A 10 W LED bulb for 100 hours: 1 kWh.

To get the cost, multiply by your rate: cost = kWh × price per kWh. At $0.18 per kWh, 1 kWh costs 18 cents.

Where to find the watts

Look for a label or plate on the back or bottom of the appliance, or check the manual. If only volts and amps are listed, multiply them: 120 volts × 10 amps = 1200 watts. For appliances with motors, this gives the maximum, so the real average is often lower.

Why real use can differ from the label

  • Cycling: fridges, air conditioners, and heaters with thermostats switch on and off, so they draw full power only part of the time. In the calculator, enter only the hours (or percent of the day) it actually runs.
  • Settings: low, medium, and high settings use different amounts of power.
  • Standby: many electronics draw a few watts even when "off."

For a precise number, a plug-in electricity usage meter can measure exactly how many kWh a plug-in appliance uses over a day or a week.
